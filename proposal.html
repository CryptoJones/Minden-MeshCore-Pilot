A Zero-Cost Pilot for Resilient Emergency Communications

Adding an off-grid radio relay to an existing tornado-siren pole so first responders and residents stay connected when cellular and power infrastructure fail — equipment donated, co-located on emergency infrastructure the city already maintains.

1The request, in one sentence

I am asking the Council to authorize a single-site pilot: permission to mount one small, solar-powered emergency-communications relay on an existing city-owned tornado-siren pole at [siren location / cross-streets]. I will purchase all equipment at my own expense and donate it to the city; installation would be performed by a city crew using the bucket truck, ideally folded into routine siren maintenance. The relay would be city property and serve the fire department's emergency-communications needs.

2The gap this closes

Our emergency response depends on infrastructure that fails in exactly the moments we need it most. Cellular networks congest or go dark in a major storm, flood, or wildfire; the towers themselves lose power once backup batteries drain. When that happens, coordination between responders and the ability to reach isolated residents degrades precisely when lives are on the line.

The city has no independent, off-grid communications layer that keeps working when the grid does not. This pilot introduces one — cheaply, and without touching a single existing system.

3The proposed solution

The relay uses Meshtastic, an open-source system built on long-range LoRa radio. It operates in the license-free 900 MHz band — FCC Part 15, no license required, no interference with public-safety radio. Key properties for municipal use:

  • Off-grid and self-powered. A small solar panel and battery run the node indefinitely with no connection to the power grid and no metered draw.
  • No recurring cost. No SIM cards, no data plans, no subscriptions. Once installed, operating cost is effectively zero.
  • Long range from elevation. Mounted high on a pole with clear line of sight, a single relay can cover several kilometers, linking handheld units carried by responders across town.
  • Encrypted and self-healing. Messages are AES-encrypted and hop automatically between nodes, so the network routes around a failed link as it grows.
  • Co-located with existing warning infrastructure. Mounting on a siren pole puts the relay on a tall, well-sited structure the city already owns and services — adding emergency communications to a pole that already carries emergency alerting.
  • Extensible. One relay is the seed of a wider low-cost network — additional nodes, remote sensors (water levels, gauges), and neighboring-agency links can be added over time.

The only cost to the city is in-kind labor: a short bucket-truck visit by a city crew to mount the unit. I've named this openly rather than call the pilot “free” — it is best scheduled alongside routine siren maintenance so the marginal time is minimal.

Optional — field units for responders. The relay is useful on its own, but its value grows when responders carry handheld radios that talk through it. These are entirely the city's option and are not part of the donated pilot: compact handheld mesh units run roughly $50–70 each, and the department could equip as few or as many responders as it wishes, at whatever pace suits the budget. I am funding my own unit regardless; this line simply lets the city buy in at any scale it chooses, or none at all while it evaluates the relay.

5Installation, safety & the siren

  • The siren comes first, always. The relay is entirely separate from the siren and its control system — its own solar power, its own antenna, no shared wiring. Placement and mounting will be set so nothing interferes with the siren's operation, activation signal, or servicing. If any conflict is ever found, the relay yields.
  • Installation: performed by a city crew with the bucket truck, on the city's schedule — ideally combined with a routine siren-maintenance visit. This is a non-energized pole, so the work is straightforward and low-risk. I will not perform pole work myself.
  • Ownership: the equipment becomes city property upon installation, documented by a brief memorandum of understanding, with agreed terms for maintenance and for removal if the pilot is discontinued.
  • Footprint: compact, weatherproof, and passive — no moving parts, no grid connection, no ongoing draw on city resources.

6Phase 2 — where a successful pilot leads

This pilot is deliberately small: one donated node, to prove the capability at no capital cost. If it performs, it becomes the evidence base for a funded expansion — and expansion is where outside money, not the city's, does the work.

Resilient emergency communications is exactly the category that disaster- and preparedness-grant programs are written to fund. A proven pilot is a far stronger grant application than a proposal on paper. Routes worth exploring with the department and emergency management:

  • FEMA preparedness & hazard-mitigation grants (e.g. EMPG, HMGP) — commonly cover resilient-communications equipment for local agencies.
  • State homeland-security and emergency-management grants — routinely fund interoperable and backup communications.
  • Regional / county emergency-management cost-sharing, since a mesh naturally links neighboring jurisdictions.

A funded Phase 2 would add relays on additional siren poles and high points for genuine citywide, self-healing coverage — and because the department would pursue the grant, the expansion carries no personal-funding entanglement at all. The pilot's job is simply to earn that next step.

7Requested action

That the Council authorize a pilot permitting a city crew to install one donated, city-owned emergency-communications relay on the identified tornado-siren pole — subject to ethics/procurement review, a memorandum of understanding on ownership and removal, and confirmation that it will not interfere with the siren — with a defined review period after which the Council may continue, pursue grant-funded expansion, or end the pilot.
