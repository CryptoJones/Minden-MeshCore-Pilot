Proposal to the Minden City Council

A Zero-Cost Pilot for Resilient Emergency Communications

Adding an off-grid radio relay to an existing tornado-siren pole so first responders and residents stay connected when cellular and power infrastructure fail — equipment donated, co-located on emergency infrastructure the city already maintains.

Submitted by
Aaron K. Clark, Volunteer EMT
Department
Minden Fire Department — EMS
Date
City cost
Equipment donated; install by city crew

1The request, in one sentence

I am asking the Council to authorize a single-site pilot: permission to mount one small, solar-powered emergency-communications relay on the existing city-owned tornado-siren pole at the southeast corner of 3rd & Hubbard. I will purchase all equipment at my own expense and donate it to the city; installation would be performed by a city crew using the bucket truck, ideally folded into routine siren maintenance. The relay would be city property and serve the fire department's emergency-communications needs.

2The gap this closes

Our emergency response depends on infrastructure that fails in exactly the moments we need it most. In a major storm, flood, or fire, cellular networks congest and then go dark as the towers' own backup batteries drain — and radio coverage has dead zones in valleys, rural edges, and inside damaged structures. When that happens, coordination among responders and the ability to reach isolated residents degrades precisely when lives are on the line.

“But the comm center has a generator.” It does, and that matters — it keeps dispatch powered. But a generator solves a different problem than this pilot does. It cannot keep the cellular network running, so the public and off-duty responders still lose their phones. It does not extend radio coverage by a single foot into the dead zones. And it does not help if the comm center itself is taken out by the very event — a direct tornado, fire, or flood hit leaves one powered building unable to reach anyone. Generators also have limits of their own: finite fuel, start failures, and refueling logistics that collapse in a regional disaster.

What the city lacks is a distributed, off-grid communications layer with no single point of failure — one that keeps working when cell service is down, reaches into coverage gaps, and survives the loss of any one site, including dispatch. This pilot introduces exactly that, cheaply, as a complement to existing systems rather than a replacement for any of them.

3The proposed solution

The relay uses MeshCore, an open-source system built on long-range LoRa radio. It operates in the license-free 900 MHz band — FCC Part 15, no license required, no interference with public-safety radio. The relay itself is a single off-the-shelf, weather-hardened device — a Seeed Wio Tracker L1 Pro, which packs the radio, battery, and solar charging into one low-power unit — paired with an external mast antenna and solar panel. Key properties for municipal use:

  • Off-grid and self-powered. A small solar panel and battery run the node indefinitely with no connection to the power grid and no metered draw.
  • No recurring cost. No SIM cards, no data plans, no subscriptions. Once installed, operating cost is effectively zero.
  • Long range from elevation — and our terrain favors it. Range at these frequencies comes almost entirely from height and clear line of sight. In hilly country, coverage breaks up badly no matter how much power a node has. Minden sits on open, gently rolling ground, which is the favorable case — but it also means there are no natural high points to use, so an existing tall structure is the only elevation available to us. That is precisely what makes a siren pole worth asking for.
  • Compatible with the statewide network. Nebraska's mesh community has standardized on MeshCore, so a Minden relay is not an isolated island — it is a node on a network that already extends across the state, and it interoperates with anything neighboring communities, volunteers, or agencies are already running.
  • Encrypted and self-healing. Every node holds its own private key, and messages hop automatically between nodes, so the network routes around a failed link as it grows.
  • Co-located with existing warning infrastructure. Mounting on a siren pole puts the relay on a tall, well-sited structure the city already owns and services — adding emergency communications to a pole that already carries emergency alerting.
  • Group and private messaging. A node can broadcast to a shared channel everyone hears — an all-call to the whole response — and send a radio-to-radio direct message privately to a single unit. Direct messages are encrypted end-to-end between the two radios, so no relay in between can read them.
  • Messages wait for responders who are out of range. A relay can also be configured to store recent traffic and hand it over when a unit comes back into range — so a crew that was inside a structure or over a hill catches up on what it missed instead of losing it.
  • Extensible. One relay is the seed of a wider low-cost network — additional nodes, remote sensors (water levels, gauges), and neighboring-agency links can be added over time.

What one relay can and cannot promise. I want to be straight with the Council about this, because it is where pilots like this get misjudged. Radio coverage at these frequencies is not a clean circle on a map — it is shaped by terrain, buildings, and trees, and real-world deployments routinely find strong signal in one spot and none a short distance away. I am not asking you to accept a coverage figure on my word. The purpose of the pilot is to measure what coverage we actually get, honestly, and report it. If the answer is “less than hoped,” that is a useful result delivered at no cost to the city, and it tells us exactly where a second node needs to go. A pilot that produces real numbers has done its job even when the numbers are modest.

“Why MeshCore and not one of the alternatives?” The main alternative is an older, larger project called Meshtastic. MeshCore is the deliberate choice, for one reason above all others: Nebraska's mesh community has standardized on it. A relay is worth far more when it is part of a network than when it stands alone, and choosing the platform the rest of the state already runs means Minden's node can talk to neighboring communities from day one, share their coverage, and draw on their operators for support. Picking the other system would buy a slightly larger national community at the cost of being unable to talk to the county next door.

The technical fit happens to point the same direction. MeshCore routes messages along discovered paths instead of having every node rebroadcast everything, which keeps the airwaves quiet and battery draw low as the network grows — the difference matters most during an incident, when traffic spikes. It also treats a pole-mounted relay as a distinct role with its own purpose-built software, rather than a handheld set to a different mode. In fairness to the Council: MeshCore is the newer of the two and its community is smaller nationally. That is a real tradeoff, and it is outweighed here by the fact that the network we would actually be joining is a MeshCore network.

The only cost to the city is in-kind labor: a short bucket-truck visit by a city crew to mount the unit. I've named this openly rather than call the pilot “free” — it is best scheduled alongside routine siren maintenance so the marginal time is minimal.

Optional — field units for responders. The relay is useful on its own, but its value grows when responders carry portable radios that talk through it. These are entirely the city's option and are not part of the donated pilot. The recommended unit is the Seeed Wio Tracker L1 Pro (MeshCore edition), about $53 each — roughly $57 delivered — a ready-to-use handheld, no assembly:

  • Screen and joystick — read and send messages on the device itself, no phone required
  • Built-in GPS — each unit can share a responder's live location on the mesh, so incident command can see where crews are during an event
  • Rechargeable battery good for several days per charge, with USB-C and solar charging
  • Rugged case; works out of the box on US 915 MHz

The department could equip as few or as many responders as it wishes, at whatever pace suits the budget. I am funding my own unit regardless; this line simply lets the city buy in at any scale it chooses, or none at all while it evaluates the relay.

An everyday community benefit, too. The value isn't only for disasters. Once the network exists, the same inexpensive handhelds give families a way to stay in touch with their children — text messages and GPS location, with no cell phone, no SIM, and no monthly bill — anywhere the community mesh reaches. For parents who want to let their kids roam a little more freely, it's a low-cost, subscription-free alternative to handing a child a smartphone. Infrastructure the city builds for emergencies quietly earns its keep on ordinary days.

5Installation, safety & the siren

  • The siren comes first, always. The relay is entirely separate from the siren and its control system — its own solar power, its own antenna, no shared wiring. Placement and mounting will be set so nothing interferes with the siren's operation, activation signal, or servicing. If any conflict is ever found, the relay yields.
  • The site is modeled before anyone goes up. Before requesting a bucket truck, I will run the location through the public line-of-sight and propagation mapping tools to confirm the pole has usable sightlines. This costs nothing, it is done in advance, and it means the city's crew is not sent up a pole to install something at a site that was never going to work. If the modeling says the site is poor, I would rather find that out first and propose a better one.
  • Installation: performed by a city crew with the bucket truck, on the city's schedule — ideally combined with a routine siren-maintenance visit. This is a non-energized pole, so the work is straightforward and low-risk. I will not perform pole work myself.
  • Ownership: the equipment becomes city property upon installation, documented by a brief memorandum of understanding, with agreed terms for maintenance and for removal if the pilot is discontinued.
  • Footprint: compact, weatherproof, and passive — no moving parts, no grid connection, no ongoing draw on city resources.

In the interest of full transparency, two things the Council should weigh: I serve the city as an unpaid volunteer EMT with no procurement authority or financial control; and the siren pole sits on property I own (southeast corner of 3rd & Hubbard), so I could incidentally benefit from a relay sited there. It's worth noting the city already holds an easement on that site for the siren, so it has the standing right to occupy and service the pole — the relay simply adds to infrastructure the city already maintains there. I am raising this before any purchase or installation and ask that the proposal be reviewed through the city's ethics and procurement process so the arrangement is documented and approved on the record. I am glad to use an alternate city-selected site if that removes any concern.

6Phase 2 — where a successful pilot leads

This pilot is deliberately small: one donated node, to prove the capability at no capital cost. If it performs, it becomes the evidence base for a funded expansion — and expansion is where outside money, not the city's, does the work.

Resilient emergency communications is exactly the category that disaster- and preparedness-grant programs are written to fund. A proven pilot is a far stronger grant application than a proposal on paper. Routes worth exploring with the department and emergency management:

  • FEMA preparedness & hazard-mitigation grants (e.g. EMPG, HMGP) — commonly cover resilient-communications equipment for local agencies.
  • State homeland-security and emergency-management grants — routinely fund interoperable and backup communications.
  • Regional / county emergency-management cost-sharing, since a mesh naturally links neighboring jurisdictions.

Why one node is a starting point and not a finished system. A single relay gives every handheld exactly one path. If that path is blocked — by a building, by weather, by the relay itself being down — there is no alternate route, because there is nothing to route to. This is the consistent experience of volunteer mesh operators elsewhere: the first node proves the idea, and the second node is what turns it into a network that heals around a failure. Stating this plainly now is more useful than discovering it later, and it is exactly the argument a grant application needs to make.

A funded Phase 2 would add relays on additional siren poles and high points for genuine citywide, self-healing coverage — and because the department would pursue the grant, the expansion carries no personal-funding entanglement at all. The pilot's job is simply to earn that next step.

7Requested action

That the Council authorize a pilot permitting a city crew to install one donated, city-owned emergency-communications relay on the identified tornado-siren pole — subject to ethics/procurement review, a memorandum of understanding on ownership and removal, and confirmation that it will not interfere with the siren — with a defined review period after which the Council may continue, pursue grant-funded expansion, or end the pilot.
