SOLAR PANEL BENCH TEST — KLEIN MM325 Minden MeshCore Pilot // measuring an MC4 panel's real output

Establish what the panel actually delivers, so the node's charge electronics get designed against measured reality instead of a marketplace listing.
Panel under test

Sold as 200 W. An identical-looking unit is listed elsewhere as 50 W, and buyer reports put it well under its rating. The seller has since shut down.

If it is truly a 50 W panel, expect Voc 21–22 V and Isc 2.5–3.0 A.

01Gear

The meter is the easy part. Getting electrical contact with MC4 connectors is the real obstacle — MC4 is deliberately touch-proof. The male pin is shrouded and the female socket recessed, specifically so you cannot get a probe tip onto them.

Meter is indoor-rated

Klein specifies the MM325 for indoor use — no moisture, rain, or snow, operating range 32–104 °F. Carry it out, take the readings, bring it back. Don't set it in wet grass and don't leave it baking on the panel face.

02Know the meter

Three jacks, and it is manual-ranging — you pick the range, it will not pick for you, and picking wrong shows OL rather than a number.

Two features to use

Lead Alert LEDs glow green when your leads sit in the jacks matching the dial position. Glancing at them before every measurement is the single habit that prevents the mistake that kills meters.

HOLD freezes the display. Outdoors in bright sun with both hands on a panel, press HOLD and walk into the shade to read it.

03Test A — Cell area (indoors, any weather, no meter)

Silicon PV is roughly 20% efficient and peak sunlight is about 1000 W/m². A panel therefore cannot exceed about 200 W per square meter of cell area, whatever the listing claimed. This bound cannot be argued with by a support rep and does not vary with test conditions.

A1Measure the cell area — the dark rectangle of actual cells, not the whole laminate including its border.

A2Length × width, in meters, gives m².

A3Ceiling wattage ≈ area_m2 × 200.

Cell areaPhysical ceilingReading
~0.25 m² (2.7 ft²)~50 W Matches the 50 W listing. The panel is what it is; the label was the lie.
~0.5 m²~100 WStill nowhere near 200 W.
~1.0 m² or more~200 W Could genuinely be 200 W. Read the stop-condition in section 05 before measuring current.

04Test B — Open-circuit voltage (Voc)

Voc barely depends on light level — even under overcast you read within about 5–10% of true. This is the number that selects the charge controller, the regulator, and which boards are candidates at all, so it is the one worth having first.

B1RED → VΩ jack. BLACK → COM jack.

B2Dial to DC volts, 200 V range. Klein's manual says start high and step down for resolution — but note that the 20 V range shows OL on a ~21 V panel. Settle on 200 V; drop to 20 V only if the reading is comfortably below 20.

B3Connect across the two MC4 leads, nothing else attached.

B4Aim the panel square at the sun. Record the highest stable reading.

Expected

An "18 V nominal" panel reads 21–22 V open-circuit. The hazardous-voltage indicator appears above 30 V — if it lights, this is a higher-voltage panel than assumed and every downstream component needs reconsidering.

Design for January, not for today

Panel voltage rises as temperature falls, roughly 0.3% per °C below 25 °C. A Nebraska morning at −20 °C puts Voc about 13–15% higher than whatever you measure in August. Every regulator downstream must be rated for that winter peak. This is the classic way a system that ran fine all summer destroys its controller in January.

05Test C — Short-circuit current (Isc)

Here the meter becomes the short across the panel. That is safe and normal for solar, which is current-limited by physics and cannot deliver more than Isc no matter what you do to it. It is emphatically not safe on a battery or any other source.

Stop condition

If Test A put the ceiling near 200 W and Voc is 18–22 V, Isc could approach 11 A — past the MM325's 10 A limit, which blows the 69032 fuse. In that case skip this test or borrow a clamp meter.

At a real ~50 W you will see 2.5–3.0 A and there is no issue.

C1Move RED to the 10A jack. BLACK stays in COM.

C2Dial to 10 A DC. Confirm Lead Alert is green.

C3Touch the probes across the panel's two MC4 leads. Read it, press HOLD, disconnect.

C4Immediately return the RED lead to the VΩ jack.

The mistake that kills meters

Probing a voltage source while the red lead is still in the 10A jack is a dead short through the meter. It is the most common way a multimeter dies. Make "red lead goes home to VΩ" a reflex the moment a current reading ends.

Duration limit

Klein specifies that above 6 A, measurement is limited to 30 seconds, followed by 10 minutes of recovery. This panel should sit well under that — but take the reading briskly anyway. Don't leave it shorted while you go find a pen.

06The math

Peak power is not Voc × Isc. A real panel delivers a fraction of that product, the fill factor — typically 0.75–0.80 for decent monocrystalline, lower for cheap cells.

Formula

P_max ≈ Voc × Isc × 0.78

Worked: 21.4 V × 2.8 A × 0.78 ≈ 46.7 W

Cross-check against the Test A ceiling. If the electrical figure exceeds the area ceiling, trust the tape measure — physics does not vary with test conditions, and a marketplace listing does.

07What each number actually decides

Peak watts is the headline, but it is not what sizes an off-grid repeater. A LoRa node draws on the order of 0.2–0.5 W average — 5 to 12 Wh/day. Nebraska yields roughly 2–3 peak-sun-hours in December, and near zero under snow cover.

MeasurementWhat it decides
Voc Charge controller and regulator selection; which boards are viable. The most important number here.
Isc Controller current rating and wire gauge. Generously oversized for this load either way.
P_max Mostly satisfies curiosity. Even a badly underperforming panel is many times what the node can consume.
Cell area The honest bound, and the only figure nobody can dispute.

The binding constraint on a pole node is battery capacity across multi-day overcast, not panel wattage. Sizing follows once Voc is known.
